# Who Holds Your Cards? The Inventory Custody Decision Every TCG Seller Should Understand

A trading card seller can own a card without physically holding it. That distinction sounds obvious until inventory is split between a home office, a card show, a marketplace fulfillment program, and a storage box waiting to be listed.

The question is no longer simply, “How many copies do I own?” It is also, “Where is each copy, who can fulfill it, and when is it actually available to sell?”

This is becoming more relevant as marketplaces develop services that store and ship inventory on a seller's behalf. But the underlying lesson applies even to a seller operating entirely from a spare bedroom: **ownership, custody, and sellable availability are three different inventory facts.**

A current example: TCGplayer Managed Inventory

TCGplayer describes its Managed Inventory service as a program in which participating sellers send cards to its Authentication Center for intake, storage, and fulfillment. Its published seller policies cover shipment creation, card inspection, discrepancies, storage, fees, and return requests.

The program changes who physically controls the cards: a provider handles storage and outbound orders under its terms.

TCGplayer's help material also says its older Store Your Products program is not accepting new applications and directs interested sellers toward Managed Inventory. Eligibility and enrollment are subject to the current program rules; sellers should not assume every account can join immediately.

The durable lesson is not that outsourced fulfillment is inherently better. It is that transferring custody creates a new set of operational responsibilities.

Ownership is not the same as availability

Imagine a seller owns three copies of the same Pokémon card.

One copy is in a labeled box at home. Another is inside a package traveling to a fulfillment center. The third has arrived at the center but has not completed intake.

An ordinary inventory spreadsheet might show quantity three. But only one copy may be immediately available for the seller to pull and ship personally.

The other copies have different statuses. The in-transit card is not safely available for an ordinary home-fulfilled order. The received-but-unprocessed card may not yet be available through the fulfillment provider's marketplace account.

If all three are counted as sellable across every channel, the business risks selling inventory it cannot deliver.

A useful inventory record therefore needs separate fields for **owned quantity**, **physical location**, **custody status**, and **available-to-sell quantity**.

Build a custody chain, not just a quantity counter

Every physical movement should create a record. That includes moving cards from a sorting desk into storage, taking them to a show, shipping them to a fulfillment partner, and receiving returned inventory.

A practical custody chain records the card's identity, condition, finish, quantity, source location, destination, movement date, and person or service responsible.

Photographs or batch identifiers can support the record.

The point is to make discrepancies traceable.

When a card cannot be located, the business should know whether it was last counted at home, packed for shipment, accepted by a third party, or sold.

Intake accuracy matters more when someone else checks the cards

Outsourced inventory creates a second verification point.

TCGplayer's Managed Inventory policies define discrepancies when submitted cards do not match the expected inventory. Its FAQ specifies grouping submissions by game, condition, foil status, and card identity, with packing order aligned to the packing slip.

Those requirements reveal an important principle: a fulfillment center can only process inventory efficiently when the seller's submission records match the physical cards.

A scanning tool may help identify products, but it does not eliminate the need to verify language, printing, finish, condition, and quantity. Two cards with the same name may belong to different sets or have different treatments.

Verify cards before they leave the building; correcting mismatches early is simpler.

Use inventory states that reflect real events

A basic status system can prevent confusion:

- **Unprocessed:** owned but not yet identified and checked. - **Ready at home:** verified and physically available for self-fulfillment. - **Reserved:** committed to an order or another sale. - **In transit:** packed and shipped to a third-party location. - **At intake:** delivered or checked in but not yet accepted as sellable. - **Available with provider:** accepted and available through the appropriate channel. - **Return pending:** requested back but not yet physically received. - **Quarantined:** returned or disputed inventory awaiting inspection.

A card should move between states because an event occurred, not because someone assumes it probably arrived.

For example, carrier delivery and a fulfillment provider's internal check-in may be separate events. TCGplayer explicitly distinguishes a carrier delivery record from check-in at its Authentication Center.

That distinction is useful even without a fulfillment partner: a package marked delivered to your home is not the same as inventory unpacked, counted, and ready for sale.

Reconcile the physical and digital counts

Compare records against actual quantities and locations.

The simplest reconciliation asks four questions: What was expected? What was received? What was accepted? What remains available?

If ten cards were sent to a partner and nine were accepted, the missing or rejected card needs an explicit disposition. It should not silently remain in available inventory.

Likewise, if a return is requested, the inventory should not automatically reappear as available at home. It must first arrive, be inspected, and be matched to the original record.

The same principle applies to consignment, grading submissions, and card shows.

Outsourcing changes the economics of time

A third party may reduce the labor required to store, pull, package, and ship orders. That can be valuable for sellers whose available time is the main constraint.

But outsourced fulfillment also introduces receiving, storage, transaction, return, and discrepancy considerations. TCGplayer's Managed Inventory policies describe several such fee categories, and the terms may change.

The comparison should therefore include more than the postage on an individual order.

Consider the time spent preparing submissions, the risk of rejected cards, how quickly inventory becomes available, the ability to retrieve unsold stock, and whether the provider's selling channels match the seller's customer base.

Small sellers may value direct control; high-volume sellers may value fulfillment capacity more.

Neither approach is universally superior.

Avoid double-selling inventory across custody locations

A card stored with a fulfillment provider should not remain simultaneously available as a home-shipped copy unless the seller actually has another physical copy at home.

This is the same fundamental problem as cross-listing across eBay, TCGplayer, and a direct storefront, but custody makes the consequences harder to resolve.

The safest approach is to assign each physical unit or verified batch to a fulfillment location. Each channel should draw its available quantity from the location capable of fulfilling that channel's orders.

When stock moves, remove it from the old location's sellable quantity before adding it to the new one.

Synchronization tools help, but sellers must confirm the source of truth.

Think about the buyer's experience

Buyers rarely care which storage system a seller uses. They care that the correct card arrives in the advertised condition and within the promised handling window.

Custody records help make those promises reliable. They also help answer buyer questions when an order is delayed or a variant is disputed.

The same principle applies to collectors purchasing from multiple channels: a seller's professional-looking storefront is not evidence that every listed item is immediately on hand. Clear availability and fulfillment expectations matter more than presentation alone.

A reliable system makes inventory information useful to both sides of the transaction.

A small-seller starting point

You do not need warehouse software to begin.

Start with one inventory table and a controlled list of locations: home storage, show stock, third-party fulfillment, inbound shipment, and returns quarantine. Give each card or batch a stable identifier. Record movements as they happen, and reconcile exceptions before the next listing batch.

If the business later adopts a managed fulfillment program, those records become the foundation for submission files and receiving checks.

The durable seller lesson

The next operational upgrade is not always faster scanning, more listings, or another sales channel. Sometimes it is knowing precisely where the inventory is.

Ownership tells you what belongs to the business. Custody tells you who holds it. Availability tells you what can actually be sold and fulfilled.

Keep those facts separate, and a small trading card operation can grow across channels and fulfillment models without losing control of its cards.
